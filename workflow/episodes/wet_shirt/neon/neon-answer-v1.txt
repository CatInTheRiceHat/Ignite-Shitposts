Accuracy: wet porous materials darken through optical index matching and internal trapping (Lekner & Dorf,
  Applied Optics 1988). Dry cotton fibers (refractive index ~1.53) sit surrounded by air (n ~ 1.00), creating a
  large index jump that scatters light strongly off the outer fibers straight back to the eye. When water
  (n ~ 1.33) fills those inter-fiber air spaces, the smaller index jump reduces immediate surface reflection so
  light refracts deeper into the cloth; moreover, scattered light heading back out meets the smooth water-to-air
  boundary and, beyond the critical angle (~49 degrees), undergoes total internal reflection back into the weave.
  More internal bounces mean more encounters with the dyed gray fibers, so more light is absorbed and less
  escapes to your eyes. Simplified here: we omit refractive-index numbers and the terms Fresnel reflection and
  total internal reflection, show a handful of animated rays instead of dense diffuse scattering, and skip how
  deeper absorption also boosts color saturation on brightly dyed fabrics (neutral gray simply dims). Still
  variable across real fabrics: the exact share contributed by reduced surface scattering versus total internal
  reflection depends on fiber type, weave density, and wetness level, and a thick mirror-like water film can
  reflect bright specular glare at grazing viewing angles.

EXTRAS

TITLE: Why Does A Wet Patch Look Darker On A Gray Shirt?

DESCRIPTION: Why does a wet patch look darker on a gray shirt? When a backyard sprinkler splashes Sol's spotless shirt, he panics and tries to scrub the spot away, until the Narrator zooms in to show what happens to the light. On dry cloth, tiny air gaps between the fibers bounce sunlight straight back to your eyes. Water fills those gaps, lets light slip deeper inside, and traps escaping rays so the fibers absorb more of them before they can get out. The shirt never changed color at all—wet fabric just traps more light.
