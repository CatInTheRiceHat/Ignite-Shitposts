Accuracy: wet porous fabrics darken through optical index matching and internal light trapping (Lekner & Dorf,
  Applied Optics 1988). Dry cotton fibers (refractive index n ~ 1.53) sit surrounded by air (n ~ 1.00); that
  large refractive-index jump produces strong Fresnel reflection and diffuse surface scattering right off the
  outer fibers back toward the viewer. When water (n ~ 1.33) fills the inter-fiber air spaces, water's
  refractive index is far closer to cotton's than air's is, so outer fiber surfaces scatter much less light
  back and incoming light refracts deeper into the dyed weave. Light scattered back toward the outside then
  hits the smooth water-to-air boundary and, at angles beyond the critical angle (~49 degrees), undergoes total
  internal reflection back into the cloth. Each extra pass through the submerged fibers increases absorption by
  the gray dye, lowering the diffuse reflectance that reaches your eyes. Simplified here: we omit the numerical
  refractive indices (1.00, 1.33, ~1.53), Fresnel's equations, and the ~49-degree critical angle in the spoken
  narration; represent dense diffuse scattering with a handful of smooth 2D arrows; stylize total internal
  reflection over the glossy surface of Sol's wet patch in clip 05 after introducing the microscopic wet
  cross-section in clip 04; and omit how deeper path-length absorption also increases color saturation on
  brightly dyed fabrics (neutral gray simply appears darker). Still variable across real fabrics: the exact
  balance between reduced surface scattering and total internal reflection depends on fiber composition, weave
  tightness, yarn twist, and water content, and a thick smooth water film can reflect bright specular glare at
  grazing viewing angles rather than looking darker.

TITLE: Why Does A Wet Patch Look Darker On A Gray Shirt?

DESCRIPTION: Why does a wet patch look darker on a gray shirt? Sol and the Narrator find out: dry cotton scatters sunlight straight back because air bends light far less than cotton fibers do. When water fills the gaps between fibers, its closer refractive index lets light scatter less and refract deeper into the cloth. Escaping light then reflects back inside off the smooth water surface through total internal reflection, so every extra pass lets the dye absorb more light. The shirt never changed color at all; less light reaches your eyes because more of it is absorbed.
