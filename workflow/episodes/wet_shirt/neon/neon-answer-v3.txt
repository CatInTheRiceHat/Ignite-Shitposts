Accuracy: wet porous materials darken through optical index matching and internal trapping (Lekner & Dorf,
  Applied Optics 1988). Dry cotton fibers (refractive index ~1.53) sit surrounded by air (n ~ 1.00), so the large
  index jump causes strong Fresnel reflection and diffuse scattering right off the outer fibers back to the eye.
  When water (n ~ 1.33) fills those inter-fiber air spaces, water's refractive index is much closer to cotton's
  than air's is, reducing immediate surface reflection so light refracts deeper into the cloth; scattered light
  heading back outward then strikes the smooth water-to-air boundary and, beyond the critical angle (~49
  degrees), undergoes total internal reflection back into the weave. More internal bounces mean more encounters
  with the dyed gray fibers, so more light is absorbed and less escapes to your eyes. Simplified here: we omit
  refractive-index numbers and the terms Fresnel reflection and total internal reflection, show a handful of
  animated rays instead of dense diffuse scattering, and skip how deeper absorption also boosts color saturation
  on brightly dyed fabrics (neutral gray simply dims). Still variable across real fabrics: the relative share of
  reduced surface scattering versus total internal reflection depends on fiber type, weave density, and wetness
  level, and a thick mirror-like water film can add bright specular glare at grazing viewing angles.

EXTRAS

TITLE: Why Does A Wet Patch Look Darker On A Gray Shirt?

DESCRIPTION: Why does a wet patch look darker on a gray shirt? Sol and the Narrator find out: light bounces straight back out whenever it crosses between air and dry cotton fibers. Water is much more like fiber than air, so when it fills the gaps, light slips deeper into the cloth and gets bounced back inside by the smooth water surface. Each extra bounce lets the fibers absorb more light before it can escape. The shirt never changed color at all, because wet fabric just traps more of the light.
