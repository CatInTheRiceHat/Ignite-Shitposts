Accuracy: wet porous fabrics darken through optical index matching and internal light trapping (Lekner & Dorf,
  Applied Optics 1988). How strongly light scatters at an interface grows with the refractive-index contrast
  between the two materials: dry cotton fibers (refractive index n ~ 1.53) surrounded by air (n ~ 1.00) have a
  large optical mismatch, producing strong Fresnel reflection and diffuse surface scattering right off the
  outer fibers back to the eye. When water (n ~ 1.33) fills those inter-fiber air spaces, water's refractive
  index is much closer to cotton's than air's is, so outer fiber surfaces scatter far less light back and
  incoming light travels deeper into the dyed weave. Light scattered back toward the outside then meets the
  smooth water-to-air underside and, at incidence angles beyond the critical angle (~49 degrees from the normal,
  i.e., shallow angles to the surface), undergoes total internal reflection back into the cloth. Every extra
  pass past the submerged dyed fibers increases absorption by the gray dye, lowering the diffuse reflectance
  returning to your eyes. Simplified here: we omit the term refractive index, numerical indices (1.00, 1.33,
  ~1.53), Fresnel's equations, and the ~49-degree critical angle in the spoken narration; show a handful of
  smooth 2D arrows rather than dense random diffuse scattering; and skip how deeper path-length absorption also
  boosts color saturation on brightly dyed fabrics (neutral gray simply appears darker). Still variable across
  real fabrics: the relative share of reduced surface scattering versus total internal reflection depends on
  fiber chemistry, weave density, yarn twist, and wetness level, and a thick smooth water film can produce
  bright specular glare at grazing viewing angles rather than looking darker.

TITLE: Why Does A Wet Patch Look Darker On A Gray Shirt?

DESCRIPTION: Why does a wet patch look darker on a gray shirt? Sol and the Narrator find out: light scatters more when two materials are very different for light, so dry air and cotton scatter sunlight straight back out. Water bends light nearly as much as cotton does, so wet fibers scatter less and light travels deeper into the cloth. Light trying to exit then hits the water's smooth underside at shallow angles and reflects back inside through total internal reflection, so on every extra trip past the dyed fibers more light is absorbed. The cloth never changed color at all; less light reaches your eyes because more of it is absorbed.
